What to look for

They differ on temperature and on how the page introduces the practice, not on what it says:

  • A opens with a portrait and a sentence. Quietest; assumes the visitor already knows they want therapy.
  • B puts his phone number in a full-width gradient band immediately under the hero, then runs the practice as plain typographic blocks, each with its own hue. The colour is doing quiet work: it says this is somewhere you can bring anything, without a badge announcing it. Boldest of the three.
  • C puts fees front and centre in a band right under the hero. Most matter-of-fact; strongest for someone comparing therapists.
  • D is the traditional one, closest in shape to the practices he pointed at: a full navigation bar, four teasers into long sections, and local landscape rather than portraiture. His photograph appears once, small and round. Weighted for a general audience — but the psychosexual and supervision sections are written out in full, so the two audiences that need detail still get it.

All three treat psychosexual work in plain words, keep the couples/throuples/poly wording, and lead with tel: and mailto:. Each service block links to a future detail page, so the single scroll can grow into /counselling/psychosexual-therapy and friends without a redesign — see docs/information-architecture.md. Mixing is fine.

The light portrait is knocked out of its background in all three; his shoulders are cut by the edge of the original photograph, so it always sits in a frame narrower than the image and the crop is made by the frame or the viewport. The top of his head is clipped in the source photograph and is pending a retouch. The dark studio frame keeps its background, tone-curved, and is only used small and round.
